The Lamplighter's Round

Nobody in Calder Street could remember a time before Mr Ashby and his ladder.

Every evening, as the light thinned over the rooftops, he came down from the top of the hill with the ladder balanced on one shoulder and his brass lamp swinging from the other hand, and one by one the street lamps woke up behind him. Ivy Meacham had watched him from the front window since she was small enough to need a cushion to see over the sill. She was eleven now, and she still watched.

What she liked was the order of it. He never hurried and he never stopped. The ladder went up against the post, he climbed four rungs — always four — and reached in through the little glass door, and then there was a pause so short you would miss it if you blinked, and then the lamp was lit and the pavement beneath it turned gold. Then down, and the ladder onto the shoulder, and on to the next one. Eleven lamps in Calder Street. Ivy had counted them so often that the counting had stopped meaning anything.

On the third Thursday in November, the seventh lamp did not light.

Ivy sat forward. Mr Ashby came down the ladder, looked up at the dark glass, and went back up. He was up there a long time this time — long enough that a woman with a shopping basket walked underneath him without noticing anything strange, long enough that Ivy's breath fogged the window and she had to wipe it with her sleeve. When he came down he did not put the ladder on his shoulder. He stood at the foot of it with his hand flat against the post, the way you might put a hand on a horse that had gone lame.

Then he did something Ivy had never seen him do in six years of watching. He turned round and looked straight at her window.

She should have ducked. Instead she lifted her hand, half a wave, feeling her face go hot, and after a moment Mr Ashby lifted his own hand back. Then he picked up the ladder and went on to the eighth lamp, and the eighth lamp lit as it always did, and the ninth, and the tenth, and the eleventh, and the street was as gold as it ever was except for one dark gap in the middle of it like a missing tooth.

Her grandmother came in with the tea things. "You'll wear that window out."

"The seventh one's gone," Ivy said.

"Then he'll see to it tomorrow. He always does." Her grandmother set the tray down and looked out over Ivy's shoulder, and something in her face changed, though Ivy could not have said what. "Or somebody will."

"What does that mean?"

"It means they're putting the electric in at the Rothwell end," her grandmother said, "and they'll not stop there." She said it the way she said most things, as though it were a matter of no importance whatsoever, and then she went out again, and Ivy heard her put the kettle down harder than she needed to.

Ivy stayed at the window until the tea went cold. She was thinking about the pause — the small one, between the reaching in and the lighting — and how she had never once wondered what happened inside it, and how she might not have many more evenings left to find out.

Model answer

Mr Ashby comes across as careful and methodical: he climbs 'four rungs — always four' and the narrator says he 'never hurried and he never stopped', which suggests a man who has done the same job the same way for years. He also seems to care about the lamps rather than simply working on them, since he spends a long time at the seventh and rests his hand on the post as though comforting an injured animal. Despite this he is not unfriendly — when Ivy waves he lifts his own hand back, which suggests a quiet, reserved kindness.

Model answer

The grandmother never explains directly what is happening, which makes the ending feel more unsettling. She mentions 'the electric' at the Rothwell end and adds 'they'll not stop there', leaving Ivy — and the reader — to work out that the lamps in Calder Street will go the same way. Her correction of 'he'll see to it' to 'Or somebody will' quietly suggests Mr Ashby himself may not be there much longer. Although she speaks 'as though it were a matter of no importance whatsoever', she puts the kettle down 'harder than she needed to', which shows she minds more than she will say. Ivy's last thoughts turn a tiny detail — the pause before each lamp lights — into something precious, and the admission that she 'might not have many more evenings left to find out' ends the passage on a note of quiet loss.
